# Calibration checklist

A report can be factually right and still overclaim, overexplain, or sound harsher than intended. Editors and authors read tone as judgment, and a referee who overstates one point loses credibility on the rest. Run this after the verification pass.

## Overstatement

Look for words that claim more than the evidence:

| Pattern | Why it overreaches | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| "stable", "robust" for an estimate whose point value moves across checks | the sign may hold while the size does not | "stays positive, with an interval excluding zero, across..." |
| "every", "all", "never", "only" | one counterexample breaks it | check the full text, then keep or qualify |
| "points to", "drives", "shows that X causes" | mechanism language from a bundled contrast | "may play a role", "is consistent with", then say what the contrast cannot isolate |
| "falls from A to B" when the difference is not significant | implies a real change | "is B, against A originally", plus the interval |
| "likely conservative", "probably biased upward" | an inference presented as fact, possibly against the authors' own caveat | give the one-clause reason and note the authors' view |
| "concedes", "admits", "fails to" | casts a stated caveat as an admission | "notes", "states", "does not" |
| "the paper does not say X" when it does, elsewhere | an absence claim without a full search | search, then credit the authors' location |
| "requires no new data" when one request needs outside facts | feasibility overstated | "needs no new estimation" or name the exception |
| a firm "Add..." for information the authors may not have | asks them to assert something | "If data allow, add..." |
| a reviewer's interpretation of an undefined term | presented as the authors' definition | "I take it to mean..." |

Also check the reverse: an essential request buried as optional, or a real error described as "a small point".

## Crediting the authors

When the manuscript already states a caveat, say so: "As Section 4 notes, ...". The comment then asks for the caveat to reach the place where it matters, such as the abstract or the conclusion, which is usually the real issue. That reads as fair and is harder to dismiss.

## Over-explaining

- A sentence that restates the previous one in different words: delete it.
- A Major and a Minor comment making the same point: keep the fuller one and cross-refer ("see Minor Comment 4").
- Several requests about the same part of the paper (for example, three separate comments on the abstract): consider grouping them as bullets under one comment.
- Explanations of standard methods the editor knows: cut them to the clause that matters for this paper.
- Hedges stacked on hedges ("may possibly suggest"): keep one.

When the user wants a tighter report, target light trimming, about 5 to 10 percent, unless they ask for more. When they asked for few changes, do not trim; list possible cuts as optional. Keep every request, number and citation.

## Tone

- Prefer "could be clearer" to "is hard to follow", and "does not report" to "fails to report".
- Keep praise specific ("the hand-collected permit data are a real strength"), not generic.
- The recommendation should match the comments. "Major revisions, limited in scope" fits when every required change can be done without new data collection. Say which changes those are.
